# The Illusion of Pleasure

Think about the last time you really enjoyed a story.

Not the last time you consumed one. Not the last time you opened an app and scrolled through chapters because you were bored, or tired, or avoiding something. The last time you genuinely, unmistakably enjoyed a story. The kind of experience where you were fully present — where the writing or the art or the emotion of a scene landed exactly right, where you felt something real, where you closed the book or the app afterward and sat there for a moment, feeling satisfied. Nourished, even. Like you'd been given something.

Now think about the last time you opened an app just to check for updates and emerged forty minutes later, having read six chapters you can barely remember. Your eyes moved across the panels. The pages turned. Something about a confrontation, a romance beat, a cliffhanger — it's already fading. You weren't really there for most of it. You were just... doing it. Consuming. Moving through content the way a shark moves through water, because stopping wasn't an option, because the forward motion itself was the point.

Were you enjoying yourself during that second experience? Or were you just doing it?

If a friend asked you "did you enjoy that?" after the first experience, you'd say yes without hesitation. The word would feel true. It would match the memory. If they asked after the second experience, you might say yes too — but there'd be a pause. A slight hollow quality to the word. Because you know, if you're honest, that you didn't really enjoy it. You just did it. The experience was neutral at best, mildly draining at worst. The "pleasure" was thin, forgettable, almost theoretical. Something you assumed must have been there because you kept doing it, not something you actually felt.

Why have you been calling both of these things by the same name?

### The Wolf and the Tapeworm

The answer is that something has attached itself to your genuine love of stories — something that mimics the shape of enjoyment so effectively that you've stopped being able to tell them apart. And to understand how this happened, we need to talk about parasites.

Imagine a wolf. A healthy, wild wolf in its natural habitat. When the wolf is hungry, it hunts. It eats until it's full. Then it stops. The hunger is real, the eating is satisfying, and the stopping is natural. The wolf doesn't lie around thinking about its next meal. When it's full, it's full. Appetite and satisfaction exist in balance. This is what a natural relationship with consumption looks like.

Now imagine the same wolf with a tapeworm inside it.

The tapeworm doesn't announce itself. It doesn't cause pain, at least not at first. What it does is subtler and more devastating. It produces chemicals that interfere with the wolf's appetite signals. The wolf eats a full meal, but the chemical message that says "I'm satisfied now" never quite arrives — or if it does, it's too weak to register. The wolf feels hungry even when its stomach is full. It eats and eats and eats, driven by a hunger that isn't its own, and it never reaches satisfaction because the hunger was never coming from a real need. It was coming from the parasite.

To an outside observer — or even to the wolf itself — it looks like the wolf really loves eating. Look how much it eats. Look how urgently it pursues food. Look how much of its life revolves around the hunt. The wolf must be getting tremendous pleasure from all this consumption. Why else would it do it?

But the wolf isn't enjoying anything. It's being manipulated. The tapeworm hijacked its natural appetite and turned it into a loop that serves only the tapeworm's survival. The wolf isn't eating for pleasure. It's eating to quiet a signal that the tapeworm keeps sending — a false hunger that can never be permanently satisfied, because satisfying it was never the point. The point is to keep the wolf eating.

This is not a chapter about wolves.

Compulsive escapist consumption is the tapeworm riding inside your genuine love of stories. The urgent, restless, insatiable pull you feel toward fiction — the "hunger" that makes you open the app when you told yourself you wouldn't, that keeps you reading past the point of interest, that makes the end of one series feel like an emergency requiring an immediate replacement — is not your love of stories. It's the Parasite. It attached itself to something real — your capacity to be moved, absorbed, and delighted by narrative — and it hijacked the machinery. It produces a chemical facsimile of appetite, a craving that feels like desire but functions like an itch. And you, experiencing that craving over and over, have learned to call it enjoyment.

But the craving isn't enjoyment. It's the opposite of enjoyment. Enjoyment satisfies. Craving empties. Enjoyment finishes. Craving continues. Enjoyment says "that was good, I'm done." Craving says "that wasn't enough, I need more."

The Parasite survives by making you feel hungry when you're already full. And it's been doing it so long that you've forgotten what genuine fullness feels like.

### The Pleasure Test

So how do you tell the difference? How do you distinguish the Parasite's manufactured hunger from genuine appetite for a story? The craving from the enjoyment? The itch from the meal?

There's a simple test. It involves looking not just at how you feel during consumption, but at how you feel before and after. Because the Parasite's signature isn't visible in the middle of the experience — during the binge, everything feels roughly the same. The difference shows up at the edges.

Every experience of consuming fiction has three phases: anticipation, engagement, and aftermath. What happens in each phase tells you everything you need to know about whether you're experiencing genuine enjoyment or the relief of a craving.

Think about the last time you genuinely enjoyed a story. The anticipation felt good. You were looking forward to it — not urgently, not desperately, but pleasantly. There was a sense of curiosity, of appetite, of wanting to know what happens next. The feeling was warm, not itchy. Inviting, not demanding.

Then the engagement. You were present. The story held your attention because it earned it, not because you were using it to drown something out. Time passed, but you didn't feel like you were losing time. You felt like you were inside something worthwhile.

And then the aftermath. You finished — maybe the chapter, maybe the whole story — and you felt satisfied. Content. Full. You didn't immediately need the next thing. The experience was complete in itself. You carried something from it — a feeling, an insight, a moment of genuine emotional response — that lingered without demanding to be fed.

Now think about the last time you consumed fiction compulsively. The anticipation didn't feel good. It felt uncomfortable. An itch. A tension. A need. You weren't looking forward to the story. You were looking forward to not feeling the way you currently felt. The craving was the star of the show, and the craving was unpleasant. That's what cravings are — unpleasant states that demand relief.

Then the engagement. You opened the app and the uncomfortable anticipation faded. For a little while, you weren't aware of the itch. The story occupied your attention. But were you really present? Or were you just... gone? Moving through pages, eyes scanning panels, the plot happening to you rather than you receiving it? If someone had asked you in the middle of chapter four what happened in chapter two, could you have told them?

And then the aftermath. You closed the app — or finally tore yourself away — and what did you feel? Not satisfaction. Not contentment. Emptiness. A hollow, overstimulated, slightly guilty feeling. The story left no residue except the need for the next one. You didn't feel full. You felt drained. And somewhere underneath the static, the original discomfort that sent you to the app in the first place was still there, waiting, having not been addressed at all.

Apply this test to your last five sessions of fiction consumption. Be honest. How many of them passed? How many involved pleasant anticipation, present engagement, and satisfying aftermath? And how many involved uncomfortable craving, automatic consumption, and hollow aftermath?

If you're like most people caught in the escapist cycle, the answer is uncomfortable. A significant portion — maybe the majority — of what you've been calling "enjoyment" fails the Pleasure Test on at least two of the three phases. The anticipation was an itch, not an appetite. The aftermath was emptiness, not satisfaction.

And the engagement? The part in the middle where you were actually reading?

That part felt good. But it felt good specifically because it made the anticipation stop. The itch went away. The uncomfortable craving that the Parasite manufactured was temporarily quieted by feeding it. And you experienced that quieting as pleasure.



That's not pleasure. That's the end of a pain the pleasure-giver caused.

### The Flattened Story

There's something else that happens when the Parasite drives consumption. The pleasure itself gets flatter. Thinner. Less real.

Think about your most memorable experiences with fiction — the ones that genuinely moved you, that you still think about years later. Those experiences had texture. Specific scenes stand out. You remember where you were when you read them, how you felt, what line of dialogue or panel of art landed in your chest and stayed there. The emotion was real. It wasn't just triggered and discarded. It was processed, integrated, felt.

Now think about the typical compulsive session. The chapters blur together. You couldn't summarize what happened in the last three installments if someone asked. Cliffhanger shocks, romance thrills, revenge satisfactions — they fired and faded within minutes. The emotions were real in the moment, in the same way a jump scare in a horror movie is real — a reflex, a spike, immediately gone. They left no trace. No insight. No lingering feeling except the need for the next spike.

This is what the Parasite does to pleasure. It can't afford for you to be genuinely satisfied — a satisfied host stops consuming. So it keeps the pleasure shallow. Just enough to keep the pages turning. Never enough to feel like you've had enough.

Compare it to music. You've had the experience of really listening to a song — present, attentive, moved. The music fills the room and you're inside it. You've also had the experience of hearing music in a store while shopping. It was playing. The notes entered your ears. But you didn't listen. You couldn't have hummed the melody thirty seconds after it ended. The music was background noise. It was there, technically, but it didn't land.

Compulsive fiction consumption is the store music. The stories are playing. Your eyes are moving across the panels. Plot is happening, characters are talking, cliffhangers are cliffhanging. But you're not really receiving any of it. You're not present. You're not listening. You're using the story as a carrier wave for the escape, and any story with enough narrative momentum would work just as well.

The Parasite doesn't want you to enjoy the story. It wants you to keep consuming. Pleasure is incidental. Volume is the point.

And the tragedy is that this flattened, shallow, almost-theoretical pleasure is what the Big Monster now points to as evidence of how much you "enjoy" fiction. *Look how much time I spend reading. Look how many series I follow. I must really love stories.* But the volume isn't evidence of love. It's evidence of the Parasite. The wolf eating endlessly isn't a gourmand. It's a host.

### The Story That Was Never the Point

Here's a way to see the Parasite's signature even more clearly. Pay attention to what happens when you finish a series you've been compulsively consuming. Not a series you were genuinely enjoying at a natural pace — a series you were bingeing, escaping into, using as a vehicle.

The moment it ends, what do you feel?

If the pleasure was truly about that specific story — its characters, its world, its emotional arc, the particular quality of its writing or art — finishing it would feel like completing a good meal. Satisfied. Full. A moment of quiet appreciation. Maybe a little sadness that it's over, but a sadness that's also gratitude. The experience was complete. It gave you what it had to give. You're done.

But that's not what happens with the Parasite, is it?

What happens is a flicker of emptiness. A restlessness that arrives almost before the last page loads. A rapid, slightly anxious search for the next thing. *What else has this author written? What's similar? What's popular right now? What has a high rating and a lot of chapters and a premise that hits the same notes?* The end of the story doesn't feel like a completion. It feels like a crisis. A void that must be filled immediately.

This is the Parasite revealing itself. It doesn't care about this story. It never did. The story was just a vehicle for the escape — a container for the consumption. Any sufficiently engaging narrative would have served the same function. The pleasure you thought you were getting from the story was actually the relief of the craving, and the craving doesn't care what relieves it. It just cares that something does.

Imagine someone who claims to love fine dining. They talk passionately about restaurants, about chefs, about the artistry of a perfectly composed dish. But whenever they finish a meal, they immediately rush to the next restaurant, order the next dish, and start eating again. No pause. No savoring. No reflection on what they just ate. No sense of fullness, ever. The moment one plate is empty, they need another.

You'd suspect, after watching this for a while, that they don't actually love food. They love eating. The food is interchangeable. The act of consumption is the point.

That's the Parasite. When you finish a series and immediately need the next one, when the end of one story feels like a threat rather than a completion, when you can binge stories you don't even think are good because the goodness was never the point — you're not experiencing pleasure. You're experiencing the Parasite's drive to keep the host consuming.

This is why the "but I enjoy it" defense feels hollow when you really examine it. Not because you're incapable of genuinely enjoying fiction. You are. The capacity for real pleasure — for being moved, absorbed, delighted by a story — is still there. It's what the Parasite attached itself to in the first place. But the Parasite has been feeding you a diluted, mass-produced, interchangeable version of that pleasure, and you've been calling it the same thing.

### Getting Your Taste Back

There's a fear that may be surfacing as you read this. It's the Big Monster's most emotionally charged threat, and it sounds something like this: *If escapist fiction isn't giving me real pleasure, then what is? If I stop, what will I enjoy? Will life just be gray and dull and empty without the one thing that consistently makes me feel good?*

This fear is real. And it is exactly what the Parasite wants you to feel.

But let me offer you a different way of seeing what's happening. Imagine someone who's been eating candy all day, every day, for years. Not as a treat. Not as part of a balanced diet. As their primary source of calories. Their taste buds are so overloaded with sugar that they can barely register a fresh strawberry. The subtle sweetness, the complex flavor, the way it tastes like something real — all of it is invisible to them. They might conclude that strawberries are bland. That they don't enjoy fruit. That they need candy to experience pleasure.

But the candy didn't give them pleasure. It stole their ability to taste anything subtler than itself. The candy created the very deficit it claimed to fill. It raised the threshold for what counts as "pleasurable" so high that only more candy could clear it.

Now imagine that person stops eating candy. At first, it's uncomfortable. The absence of sugar feels like a loss. Nothing tastes good. Everything seems flat. This is the withdrawal phase — not evidence that candy was essential, but evidence of how thoroughly it had colonized the palate.

And then, gradually, the taste buds recover. A strawberry starts to taste like something. Not just sweet — complex. Fragrant. Alive. Real food becomes pleasurable again, not because the person "replaced" candy with fruit, but because they stopped blocking their own capacity for genuine enjoyment. The pleasure was always available. The candy was just standing in the way, insisting it was the only source.

This is what happens when the Parasite is starved and the compulsive consumption stops. Not immediately — there's a reset period, a recalibration of the appetite. But gradually, the capacity for genuine enjoyment returns. Not just enjoyment of fiction — enjoyment of everything. The quiet pleasure of a conversation without a screen in your hand. The subtle satisfaction of finishing a task you'd been avoiding. The deep, wordless contentment of simply being present in your own life without needing to escape it.

And fiction — when you choose it, when you're genuinely hungry for a story, when you're not using it to mute something else — becomes capable of giving you what it was always supposed to give you. Not escape. Not relief. Not the temporary quieting of a Parasite's manufactured hunger. But genuine enjoyment. The Saturday afternoon experience. The story that lands. The satisfaction that lasts.

You are not giving up pleasure by quitting. You are regaining your capacity to experience it.

The Parasite has been standing between you and genuine enjoyment for so long that you've mistaken its cravings for your own appetite. You've been eating candy and calling it nourishment, wondering why you never feel full. The hunger you've been trying to satisfy with fiction was never a hunger for fiction. It was a hunger the Parasite created, and fiction was just the nearest thing it could hijack to feed itself.

You don't need to find a replacement for the "pleasure" of escapist fiction. You need to see that what you've been calling pleasure was, most of the time, just the end of an itch. And an itch you don't have doesn't need scratching.
