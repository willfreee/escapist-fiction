# "I Deserve a Treat"

You've had a long day. A draining week. A difficult month. You've been working, enduring, showing up, pushing through. And now, finally, there's a gap — an evening with no obligations, an hour that belongs to you. A voice rises up, warm and persuasive.

*I deserve this. I've earned it. Let me just relax and read something.*

And you open the app. It feels like an act of kindness toward yourself. Like self-care. Like giving a hardworking person the break they've earned.

So let me ask you something. If a friend told you they "deserved" a drink after every hard day — needed it, earned it, couldn't unwind without it — would you call that self-care? Or would you start to worry?

The answer is obvious when it's someone else. But when it's you, and the substance is fiction instead of alcohol, the same pattern wears a different costume. *It's just reading*, you tell yourself. *It's not the same.* The costume works. You don't see the pattern at all.

Let's look at what's actually happening when you "reward" yourself with escapist fiction. And let's start by looking at who taught you to call it a reward in the first place.

### The Coal Miner's Bonus

Imagine a coal miner. He spends twelve hours underground, breathing dust, exhausting his body. His muscles ache. His lungs are heavy. At the end of the day, he climbs out of the darkness and someone hands him his reward — another pile of coal to carry home.

"This is your treat," they tell him. "This is what you've earned. Take it."

Would he feel rewarded? Or would he recognize that his "reward" is just more of what exhausted him in the first place, dressed up in different language? The coal is the same coal. The weight is the same weight. The only difference is that someone called it a gift instead of a shift.

Now look at your reward.

You're mentally exhausted. Cognitively drained. Your brain has been processing, deciding, performing, worrying all day. You're emotionally spent — maybe from interpersonal friction, maybe from the low-grade anxiety of modern life, maybe from holding yourself together through something difficult. Your body and mind are sending clear signals: *I need rest. I need quiet. I need to not be processing anything for a while.*

And what do you give yourself as a treat?

More cognitive load. More emotional stimulation. More mental processing. A story to follow, characters to track, plot twists to anticipate, cliffhangers to react to. You're rewarding an exhausted brain with more of what exhausted it — just in narrative form. The coal miner coming home and opening another sack of coal, calling it his bonus.

This is the Magic Trick in its most personal, intimate form. The magician exhausts you — the habit itself contributes to your depletion by consuming your rest time, your sleep, your mental bandwidth — and then steps forward with a solution. *You've worked so hard. You deserve to escape. Here, let me give you what you've earned.* And the "gift" is the thing that's been depleting you. The "reward" is the continuation of the drain.

A genuine reward restores you. It leaves you feeling better afterward than you did before. It replenishes what was spent. A nap is a reward. A walk outside is a reward. A real conversation with someone you love is a reward. A good meal, eaten slowly, is a reward. These things give back. They return resources to your depleted system.

Escapist fiction takes more. It consumes the last reserves of your attention, your energy, your time. It leaves you foggy, drained, overstimulated, and often guilty about how long you spent. The exhaustion you were "rewarding" yourself for is now deeper than it was before. You didn't give yourself a treat. You gave yourself another shift in the coal mine and called it a prize.

*But it feels good in the moment*, you might be thinking. *I enjoy it. That has to count for something.*

Does it? If you gave a child a "treat" that made them feel sick, tired, and disappointed in themselves afterward — and the child kept asking for it because the first few bites tasted sweet — would you keep handing it to them? Or would you recognize that the treat wasn't treating them well?

You are the child and the adult in this scenario. The part of you that reaches for fiction believes it's getting a reward. It feels the sweetness of the first few minutes — the relief, the absorption, the muting of the day's tension. The part of you that lives with the aftermath knows it isn't a reward. It knows about the fog, the fatigue, the guilt, the way the original exhaustion is still there waiting when the story ends. One part of you is asking for candy. The other part knows the candy is making you sick. Which part are you going to believe?

### Who Wrote the Script?

There's another layer to the reward story. You probably think it's yours. That you independently arrived at the conclusion that fiction is a good way to treat yourself — a personal preference, a quirk of your personality, something you discovered on your own.

But think about where you learned to equate "treating yourself" with "consuming something."

The message is everywhere. It's in the advertising that tells you you've earned a break — and the break always involves buying something, watching something, consuming something. It's in the self-care industrial complex that repackaged consumer spending as wellness and sold it back to you in pastel colors. You deserve the bath bomb. You deserve the streaming subscription. You deserve the glass of wine, the new release, the premium upgrade. You've been working hard. Reward yourself.

The message is so pervasive that it's become invisible. It just feels like common sense. Of course you deserve a treat. Of course consumption is how you treat yourself. What else would a reward be?

But here's the thing. Who benefits from that message? Who profits when you believe that the best way to care for yourself is to consume something? The companies selling the bath bombs. The platforms serving the ads. The algorithms optimized to keep you watching, reading, scrolling. The entire commercial ecosystem that depends on your belief that consumption equals self-care.

If a casino put up a sign that said "You've worked hard — you deserve to gamble," you'd recognize the manipulation instantly. The entity telling you to reward yourself is the same entity profiting from your "reward." You'd see the conflict of interest. You'd distrust the message.

But when the recommendation algorithm tells you the same thing — *you've been working hard, here's exactly the story you need, just one more chapter, you deserve this* — you don't see the casino. You see a helpful service. A treat. Something you earned.

The "reward" narrative isn't a personal truth you discovered. It's a commercial script you were handed, and the Parasite happily adopted it as its own voice. *I deserve this* sounds like self-compassion. It's actually the Parasite speaking in the first person, using the language of wellness to justify the very thing that's depleting you.

A genuine reward doesn't come from the same system that creates the depletion. If you're exhausted by your relationship with your phone, a reward that involves more phone time isn't a reward. If you're drained by the constant cognitive load of modern life, a reward that involves more cognitive load isn't a reward. The magician who exhausted you is now offering you a prize — and the prize is more exhaustion, gift-wrapped in the language of self-care.

### The Comfortable Prison

Now let's talk about the safety net. The argument you reach for when all the other justifications start to wobble. The one that feels unassailable.

*Okay, maybe it's not a perfect reward. Maybe it's not even good for me. But at least it's just reading. At least I'm engaging with stories instead of drinking, or doing drugs, or doomscrolling social media, or gambling. It could be so much worse. Fiction is a wholesome vice. It's harmless. I'm not hurting anyone.*

I want you to notice what this argument does. It doesn't defend the behavior. It compares it to worse behaviors and claims immunity by association. It's not an argument that fiction is good for you. It's an argument that fiction isn't as bad as heroin. And from there, it slides — almost invisibly — to the conclusion that fiction doesn't deserve scrutiny at all.

This is the Parasite's favorite defense. Not "this is serving me." Not "this is making my life better." Just "this isn't the worst thing I could be doing." And because you accept that frame, you never ask the question that actually matters.

Imagine two people trapped in separate rooms. The first room has bars on the windows. The person inside can see they're trapped. They know they need to escape. The second room has no bars. The windows are open. The door is unlocked. But the room has a very comfortable chair, and an endless supply of engaging stories, and no one is forcing the person to leave. The person in the second room never tries the door handle. Why would they? The chair is comfortable. The stories are good. No one is telling them they have to go.

Which person is more trapped? The one who can't leave, or the one who doesn't realize they can?

The "wholesome vice" defense is the comfortable chair. It's the open window you never look at because the room doesn't feel like a prison. There are no obvious consequences. No rock bottom. No intervention.  Just a quiet, comfortable, culturally approved consumption pattern that slowly consumes your time, your attention, your energy, your capacity for real rest and real connection and real life — all while you tell yourself it's harmless.

In some ways, the second room is the more dangerous one. At least the person with bars on the windows knows they need to escape. The person in the comfortable chair can stay there for years, decades, a lifetime — never realizing the door was unlocked the whole time.

The question isn't "is escapist fiction better than heroin?" The question is "is this pattern actually serving me, or is it quietly consuming my life while I tell myself it's harmless?" Those are different questions. The Parasite wants you to answer the first one, because the answer is obviously yes, and that yes feels like a vindication. It doesn't want you to ask the second one at all.

### The Halo Effect

There's a subtler version of the wholesome vice defense. It's not just that fiction is "not as bad" as other compulsions. It's that fiction — because it involves reading, because it involves stories, because it's associated with culture and imagination and art — actually carries a kind of virtue. A halo.

*At least I'm reading. Reading is good for you. It builds empathy. It expands vocabulary. It exercises the imagination. It's a sign of intelligence. People who read are smarter, more creative, more emotionally intelligent. I'm not wasting time. I'm enriching myself.*

You've probably felt this. That small glow of superiority when you compare your habit to someone else's. *I'm not scrolling TikTok for hours. I'm not watching trash reality TV. I'm reading. I'm engaging with narrative. I'm learning about different worlds and perspectives.* It feels good to believe this. It transforms the compulsion from something you're doing to yourself into something you're doing for yourself. Self-improvement by proxy.

Here's the sleight of hand. Yes, reading can be enriching. Yes, stories can build empathy and expand perspective and exercise the imagination. But those benefits come from a specific kind of engagement — present, attentive, reflective. They come from reading something and then thinking about it. Digesting it. Letting it change you. They don't come from bingeing six isekai chapters at 1 a.m. with your eyes half-closed, barely registering the plot, already reaching for the next one before the current one ends.

You know the difference between the Saturday afternoon session where you really read something — present, attentive, moved — and the late-night spiral where the chapters blur together and you couldn't summarize what happened if someone asked. The "reading is virtuous" halo belongs to the first experience. The Parasite steals it and wears it over the second, convincing you that because both involve words on a screen, both are equally enriching.

If someone ate nothing but cotton candy for every meal and defended it by saying "at least I'm eating — food is necessary for survival, eating is healthy," you'd recognize the logical sleight of hand immediately. The fact that food is valuable doesn't make cotton candy nourishing. The category "food" contains both salmon and sugar. The category "reading" contains both deep literary engagement and compulsive escapist bingeing. They are not the same activity. They don't produce the same effects. They don't deserve the same halo.

The Parasite loves this confusion. It's how it hides in plain sight. It wraps itself in the genuine value of stories — the real power of narrative, the real beauty of art, the real human need for meaning and imagination — and uses that wrapping to deflect scrutiny from the compulsive pattern underneath. You're not defending a compulsion. You're defending literature. You're defending art. You're defending the human capacity for imagination. How could that be a problem?

It's a problem because the compulsion isn't literature. It's not art. It's not imagination. It's the Parasite using those things as a vehicle for escape, and borrowing their cultural prestige to avoid being seen for what it is. The medium is not the behavior. The value of stories is not the value of compulsive consumption. And the Parasite's ability to blur that distinction is the only reason it's survived this long.

### What You Actually Deserve

So let's return to where we started. That voice at the end of a long day. *I deserve this.*

You're right. You do deserve something. You deserve genuine rest. You deserve real restoration. You deserve to feel better after your break than you did before it. You deserve a reward that actually rewards you — that replenishes what was spent, that leaves you clearer and calmer and more capable, that treats you the way you would treat someone you genuinely care about.

What you don't deserve is more depletion dressed up as a gift. You don't deserve a "treat" that leaves you foggy, drained, and guilty. You don't deserve a "reward" that deepens the very exhaustion it claims to address. You don't deserve the coal miner's bonus — another sack of the same weight, handed to you with a smile and a lie about what you've earned.

The Parasite has been speaking in the voice of self-care for so long that you've forgotten what real care sounds like. Real care doesn't say "you've earned the right to escape your life." Real care says "you've earned the right to be present in your life — fully, vividly, without needing to numb yourself to it." Real care doesn't offer you a comfortable chair in an unlocked room. Real care reminds you that the door is open, and the chair was never as comfortable as it claimed to be.

You deserve to be free. Not rewarded with fiction. Free from the need for it. That's what you've actually earned. And that's what this book is going to give you — not as a treat, not as a reward for good behavior, but as the natural consequence of seeing clearly what was always there.

The comfortable chair is an illusion. The door has always been unlocked. And the voice that says *I deserve this* was never yours. It was the Parasite, speaking in the first person, asking you to stay just a little longer. You don't owe it anything. You've already given it enough.
